\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:bg:motivation}

The process of making handmade paper of the kind used in late medieval
and early modern Europe is as follows: a vatman dips a wooden frame
with metal wires in between, called a mould, into a vat of macerated
fibre solution, lifts it out, and lets the water drain through the
frame's mesh~\cite[ch.~1]{darold2020paper}. The mould carries two sets
of perpendicular wires. A small number of stout chain wires, typically
spaced two to three centimetres apart, run from one short edge to the
other and provide structural support. On top of them, a much denser
bed of fine laid wires, of the order of eight to fifteen per
centimetre, runs perpendicular to the chain wires. As the wet pulp
settles, it deposits more material in the spaces between wires than
directly over the wires themselves, so that the dried sheet ends up
with a periodic thickness pattern that mirrors the wire layout. Held
up to a strong light, this pattern appears as a series of pale and
dark lines that is visible to human eyes.

Each mould was made by hand, with wires drawn and tied by an individual
artisan, so no two moulds are exactly identical. Moreover, paper was
almost always made in pairs: two near-identical moulds were used in
alternation by the same vatman to keep production continuous while
each sheet was being couched. The result is that every batch of paper
from a given workshop carries a recognisable, two-state signature in
the geometry of its laid and chain lines~\cite[ch.~2]{darold2020paper}.
Codicologists study this signature to group folios that come from the
same mould or mould pair, and from there to assign manuscripts to a
region, a workshop, or even a specific year.

To this day, the analysis of laid-line features is still largely
manual. A researcher inspects each folio under raking or transmitted
light, counts wires along a short ruler, and records a single density
figure in lines per centimetre. The procedure is slow,
observer-dependent, and exposes fragile paper manuscripts to repeated
handling. It also discards almost all of the information present in
the original image: the variation of density across the page, the
local orientation of the laid lines, and the distribution of wire
diameters are all collapsed to a single scalar. At the same time,
major research libraries -- including Cambridge University Library,
the Bodleian, and the Bibliothèque nationale de France -- now
routinely capture multispectral image stacks of their
holdings~\cite{TODO_msi_libraries}. These stacks contain far more
information than a single density estimate, but no robust automated
pipeline yet exists to turn them into the quantitative
mould-fingerprint data that codicology actually requires. Closing
that gap is the central aim of this project.

\subsection{Current Feature Extraction Techniques}
\label{sec:bg:prior_extraction}

Automated extraction of laid and chain lines from digital images of
historical paper has a small but established literature. Most existing
techniques operate on transmitted-light or radiographic images,
treating the folio as a two-dimensional signal with a dominant spatial
frequency. The earliest systematic study, by Hiary and
Ng~\cite{TODO_hiary_ng}, applied projection-based Fourier methods to
thresholded transmitted-light scans, extracting the laid-line density
from the dominant peak in a one-dimensional power spectrum of the
column sum. Subsequent variants substituted Gabor filter
banks~\cite{TODO_gabor_paper}, autocorrelation, or wavelet
decompositions. These methods are simple to implement and reasonably
robust to global illumination, but they share three common weaknesses:
the line orientation must be supplied externally or estimated as a
separate pre-processing step, the spectrum is computed over the whole
image and therefore averages out local variations from curvature or
ink coverage, and no uncertainty figure accompanies the extracted
density.

A second strand of work has grown out of large-scale bibliographic
projects such as the Bernstein and WIES
initiatives~\cite{TODO_bernstein,TODO_wies}, which catalogue watermarks
across European collections. The emphasis there has been on capturing
high-quality transmitted-light images and on building searchable
databases of watermark designs, with comparatively little attention to
the periodic chain-and-laid background that surrounds the watermark.
Pipelines in this strand tend to be tuned per-image by a human
operator rather than run as a single automated process. Radiographic
methods such as the chain-line detection of Van der
Lubbe~\cite{TODO_vanderlubbe}, which combines morphological filters
with vertical projections, give cleaner signals but require expensive
imaging that is out of reach for most institutions.

The transmitted-light and radiographic modalities used by these prior
methods share a more fundamental limitation: they collapse the
three-dimensional micro-relief of the folio surface onto a single
two-dimensional intensity image. The micro-relief itself, in which the
laid wires leave subtle ridges and valleys on each side of the page,
carries information that is invisible to flat-lit imaging. The
following section describes the alternative imaging modality on which
this project is built.

\subsection{Paper Imaging using Directional Reflectance Microscopy}
\label{sec:bg:drm}

% --- DRM apparatus paragraph --
The data analysed in this report were acquired using a directional
reflectance microscope (DRM) developed by the AddME Lab in the
Department of Engineering at the University of Cambridge. The
instrument fixes a camera above a sample stage and steps a
controllable light source through a regular grid of elevation and
azimuth angles, capturing one image per direction. The original
purpose of the DRM was to characterise the surface texture of
crystalline metallic samples~\cite{TODO_seita_drm}, and its first
application to handmade paper is recent.
% TODO: insert Figure -- DRM hardware schematic or photograph
% (cf. Andrew Fig 3, who simply uses an iPad sketch).

% --- DRP definition paragraph --
For each spatial pixel $(x, y)$ on the folio, the DRM produces a
sampled reflectance function $I(x, y; \theta, \phi)$ where $\theta$
is the elevation angle of the source measured from the page normal
and $\phi$ is its azimuth in the plane of the page. The full
collection of these per-pixel functions across the image is called
the directional reflectance profile (DRP) stack. Visualised at a
single pixel, a DRP appears as an annular heat map: the outer
ring corresponds to grazing illumination, the inner ring to
near-normal illumination, and the angular coordinate around the
annulus encodes the source azimuth.
% TODO: insert Figure -- DRP annular plot example
% (cf. Andrew Fig 4: pixel DRP for a single laid-line peak).

% --- Why DRP suits laid-line detection paragraph --
The DRP is well suited to laid-line analysis because the laid-line
modulation of the paper surface is too subtle to read off a flat-lit
image but produces a clear directional signature in reflected light.
Under uniform illumination, the thickness variation
caused by the laid wires gives only weak greyscale contrast. Viewed
from a grazing angle aligned with the line normal, however, the same
modulation casts a strong, directional shadow that is easy to see.
A DRP records the full $(\theta, \phi)$ dependence of that shadow in
a single acquisition, so a detector built on top of a DRP can find,
rather than be told, the orientation and period at which the
laid-line signal is strongest. This property is what makes the
present pipeline simpler to operate than the projection-based
methods of the previous section, where orientation has to be
supplied as an external parameter.

\subsection{Manuscript Dataset}
\label{sec:bg:dataset}

The folios analysed in \Cref{sec:benchmark} were drawn from the
Mapping Paper in Medieval England corpus curated by Professor
Orietta Da Rold and the Manuscripts Lab in the Faculty of English at
Cambridge~\cite{darold2020paper}. The corpus comprises 5841
manuscripts of which at least 736 are written on
paper~\cite{TODO_manuscripts_lab_mapping}; from these, nine folios
spanning four manuscript stocks were selected for the present
benchmark. The shelfmarks used in the report follow the Cambridge
University Library convention and are summarised in
\Cref{tab:dataset_summary} together with the spreadsheet ground-truth
density recorded by the Manuscripts Lab for each stock. Two of the
nine folios (Kk1-5 f5v and Kk1-5 f9v) were additionally counted by
hand on the original folio, providing a higher-confidence calibrated
ground truth that is used in \Cref{sec:benchmark:manual_gt} to
estimate the systematic accuracy of the pipeline.

\subsection{Pipeline Overview}
\label{sec:bg:contributions}

The work reported here treats the following inverse problem. Given
a DRP stack $I(x, y, \theta, \phi)$ together with the imaged
field-of-view width in centimetres, the pipeline must return three
quantities: the laid-line density $\rho$ in lines per centimetre,
the local wire diameter $w$ expressed as a full-width-at-half-maximum
in millimetres, and the local laid-line orientation $\alpha(x, y)$
in degrees, along with an associated uncertainty for each. The
pipeline must operate with no per-folio parameter tuning beyond the
optical scale, and must report enough internal evidence for a codicologist
 to judge whether to trust each estimate.

The developed pipeline is organised as five processing
stages, illustrated in \Cref{fig:pipeline_overview}. The loader
processes the raw DRM image stack by applying a background subtraction
to remove illumination non-uniformity. The direction-estimation
stage reduces the four-dimensional stack to two two-dimensional maps
that record, per pixel, how directional the local reflectance lobe
is and the azimuth in which it points. The trigonometric patch
enhancement stage uses those direction maps to construct an
enhanced greyscale image in which laid lines stand out strongly
against the background. The detection stage applies a frequency
estimator, a spectral-power detector
with dual-band cross-validation, to the enhanced image and returns
the laid-line period and orientation. The evaluation stage finally
computes a small bundle of quality metrics: the coefficient of
determination of a harmonic model fitted to the detected lines, a
self-contrast z-score against a null distribution of randomly rotated
images, a split-half stability measure, and the per-segment wire
full-width-at-half-maximum distribution. The five stages are
described in turn in \Cref{sec:direction,sec:detection}, and the
evaluation metrics are defined in \Cref{sec:detection:evaluation}.

Three contributions of this project deserve highlighting. The first
is the staged DRP-based pipeline itself, which to the author's
knowledge is the first automated pipeline to recover laid-line
density, orientation and wire diameter from a DRP stack
without per-folio parameter tuning. The second is a synthetic
phantom characterisation framework that supports four orthogonal
sweeps (period, signal-to-noise ratio, orientation, and wire width)
over a controllable line-pattern generator, and that is used in
\Cref{sec:benchmark:phantom} to map the failure modes of the
detector one variable at a time before applying it to real data.
The third is the nine-folio benchmark introduced in
\Cref{sec:bg:dataset}, which together with the manual ground truth
on two folios provides strong quantitative accuracy figures
yet reported for an automated laid-line pipeline on real manuscript
material.

Subsequent to this section, \Cref{sec:direction} describes the direction-estimation and
