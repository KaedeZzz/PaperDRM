\section{Initial Detection Attempts}
\label{sec:first_attempts}

% PAGE TARGET: ~3 pages.
% Andrew-style failure narrative: two approaches that did not work or did
% not work well, leading to the revised multi-phi method of Section 3.

Two approaches attempted to extract laid lines from a DRP stack: the first one 
exploited the per-pixel directional information directly and built post-processing on top of it; while that
approach turned out to be biased, a second approach was tried by applying a Fourier estimator on a single post-processed grazing-light image.
That second approach worked on clean folios but proved fragile on the
real benchmark. The two attempts are described here in turn, both
because they shaped the design of the final pipeline in
\Cref{sec:multiphi}.

\subsection{Direct DRP per-pixel analysis}
\label{sec:first_attempts:legacy}

The first detector reduced each pixel's DRP to a dominant azimuth. For pixel $(x, y)$, the
reflectance values $I(x, y, \theta, \phi)$ were first averaged over
elevation $\theta$ to give a one-dimensional profile in azimuth. The
mean was subtracted and the first-order Fourier coefficient of the
residual was taken as a complex number $X + i Y$. The magnitude
$\sqrt{X^2 + Y^2}$ measured how strongly that pixel lies toward one
azimuth, and the angle $\mathrm{atan2}(Y, X)$ gave the dominant
direction. The output of the stage was therefore two maps with the
same spatial size as the folio: one of anisotropy strength and one of
azimuth.

A simple direction map of this kind is too noisy to use as the input
to a frequency detector. On the one hand, the inks, stains, and surface unevenness
of real manuscripts produce strong local anistotropies and noises that largely dominants
the response in comparison of the laid lines. Moreover, the laid lines themselves are not perfectly straight and aligned,
so lines in different regions can have varying local directions, and a global detector would not find a single dominant pattern.
Thus, the next stage therefore tiled the page with overlapping square patches of 512 pixels and computed, in each patch, the circular mean
direction. Each pixel then received a Gaussian likelihood of agreeing
with the patch direction, and the patch responses were blended across
the page using a Hann window. The output was a greyscale image in
which the laid-line orientation locally dominated and which could be
fed to a frequency detector.

The frequency detector was a patchwise Gabor bank scanning periods
from 4 to 80 pixels. For each patch, the period at which the
filter response was strongest was taken as the local laid-line
period, and a global density estimate was formed by computing the weighted sum of the
patch responses. On synthetic phantoms with a known period, this
pipeline consistently reported half of the true period; the
underlying cause was traced to two biases in the Gabor
stage. From a Fourier analysis perspective, the squared (absolute) Gabor response doubles the fundamental
frequency of any input that crosses zero, so a clean sinusoidal
laid-line signal appears at twice its true frequency in the squared
response. Moreover, the proportional bandwidth of the Gabor bank meant that
the higher-frequency response was also scored more favourably than
the true peak. The combination locked the detector onto the wrong
harmonic.

The lesson from this attempt was straightforward. The DRP carries
useful directional information, but reducing it to per-pixel azimuth
maps before doing frequency analysis throws away too much of the
signal-to-noise on the way through. The revised pipeline goes back
to the raw images and uses the DRP only as a way to obtain several
independent grazing-light views of the same folio.

\subsection{Single grazing-light image with radial FFT}
\label{sec:first_attempts:simple}

The second attempt bypassed the direction map and the patchwise
enhancement altogether. From the DRP stack a single grazing-light
image was extracted at the steepest available elevation, and the
one-dimensional power spectrum of the column-mean of that image
(after rotation so that the laid lines ran vertically) was
computed. The dominant peak in the spectrum, restricted to a
plausible search band of eight to eighty pixels, gave the laid-line
period directly. A single Gabor filter at the detected period was
then applied to clean the one-dimensional signal, the phase was
fitted to find the wire positions, and a grid was overlaid on the
image. Wire diameter was estimated from the harmonic amplitudes of
the broadband signal using a Gaussian-comb model.

This pipeline is short and fast, and on a clean synthetic phantom it
recovers the period to better than one per cent. On real folios it
is also accurate when the chosen grazing image happens to be the one
in which the laid lines are most visible. Its weakness is that the
choice of grazing image is essentially arbitrary: depending on the
folio surface curvature, the azimuth of the chosen view, and the
location of ink coverage, one grazing image can give a clean peak
while another gives mostly noise. The simple pipeline therefore
trades the bias of the legacy approach for a single-frame brittleness
that is hard to predict in advance.
