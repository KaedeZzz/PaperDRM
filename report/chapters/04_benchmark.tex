\section{Validation and Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

The pipeline is evaluated on two parallel benchmarks that reflect
the two input modalities it supports.
\Cref{sec:benchmark:drp} reports the DRP benchmark: four small
field-of-view datasets acquired with the AddME Lab directional
reflectance microscope and processed by the multi-phi DRP variant
of \Cref{sec:multiphi:multi}. \Cref{sec:benchmark:msi} reports
the MSI benchmark: a synthetic phantom characterisation and nine
real folios from the Mapping Paper in Medieval England corpus,
all processed by the single-image MSI variant of
\Cref{sec:multiphi:single}. \Cref{sec:benchmark:ablation} closes
with a short ablation on the line-direction detector that the
two variants share.


% ==========================================================================
\subsection{DRP benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark:drp}

The DRP benchmark consists of four datasets acquired at a field
of view of approximately 8.65 cm: one controlled hand-made
reproduction made by Professor Da Rold, used here as a known-mould
reference, and three zoomed-in scans of two folios drawn from the
Mapping Paper corpus. All four were processed by the multi-phi DRP
variant of \Cref{sec:multiphi:multi}; the per-image phase fit and
the split-half stability metric of \Cref{sec:multiphi:evaluation},
which are only well defined on multi-image input, are available
here and are reported alongside the other four members of the
metric bundle.


\subsubsection{Controlled reproduction: dataset 10}
\label{sec:benchmark:drp:darold}

Dataset 10 is a sheet of paper hand-made by Professor Da Rold
using a reproduction of a fifteenth-century European mould.
Because the paper was formed on a mould of known geometry, it is
the closest the benchmark comes to a true ground truth at the
level of mould specification rather than at the level of a
hand-count on the dried sheet. The pipeline reports a period of
$56.1$ pixels on this dataset, corresponding to a density of
$8.31$ lines per centimetre at the optical scale of the
acquisition. The five-metric bundle is healthy: the self-contrast
$z$-score is $+4.05$, the harmonic-fit $R^{2}$ at four harmonics
is $0.35$, the split-half period standard deviation is below
$0.001$ pixels, and the per-segment wire full-width-at-half-maximum
has an interquartile range of $[0.33, 0.43]$ mm around a median
of $0.38$ mm.

The density and the wire diameter that the pipeline reports for

report. They are within the ranges seen on the strongest folios

the pipeline behaves consistently across the change of optical
scale. Dataset 10 is therefore the natural primary reference
against which the rest of the benchmark should be read.


\subsubsection{Zoomed-in real folios: datasets 2, 2b and 4}
\label{sec:benchmark:drp:zoomed}

Datasets 2, 2b and 4 are zoomed-in DRP acquisitions of two real
folios from the Mapping Paper corpus, acquired at the same
field-of-view as dataset 10 but on aged manuscript material with
varying degrees of ink coverage and surface unevenness. They
illustrate two practical points about deploying the multi-phi
variant on imperfect inputs.

Datasets 2 and 2b are different crops of the same source
acquisition. The unrestricted full-frame run (dataset 2) reports
a period of $74.5$ pixels with a self-contrast $z$-score of only
$+0.36$ and a harmonic-fit $R^{2}$ of $0.002$. Both metrics are
characteristic of a detection that has locked onto noise rather
than the real laid-line signal. Re-running the same acquisition
with a crop restricted to a quadrant of the image with light ink
coverage (dataset 2b) leaves the recovered period essentially
unchanged but raises the self-contrast $z$-score to $+3.35$ and
the harmonic-fit $R^{2}$ to $0.10$. The density figure that
emerges from dataset 2b is therefore the trustworthy one for this
folio, and the recipe -- crop to a quieter region of the folio
before running the pipeline -- generalises to any acquisition
whose full-frame self-$z$ is low.

Dataset 4 is a separate zoomed-in folio acquired in the same
session. Here the configuration specifies a period search range
of $0.017$ to $0.106$ cm, equivalent to 8 to 50 pixels at the
acquisition scale, in order to exclude the long-period band where
the dataset 2 failure occurred. With that constraint the pipeline
reports a period of $40.2$ pixels, a density of $11.3$ lines per
centimetre, a self-contrast $z$-score of $+2.08$ and a
harmonic-fit $R^{2}$ of $0.095$. The metric bundle is weaker than
on dataset 10 but uniformly stronger than on dataset 2, and the
recovered density is consistent with the densities of the
high-quality folios in the MSI benchmark of
\Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:manual_gt}. The dataset 4 result is the
clearest demonstration that the per-folio period range, in
combination with a sensible crop, is sufficient to recover a
trustworthy density from a real folio at the zoomed-in scale.

\subsection{MSI benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark:msi}

The MSI benchmark consists of a synthetic phantom characterisation
followed by a nine-folio real-folio benchmark, both processed by
the single-image MSI variant of \Cref{sec:multiphi:single}. The
phantom characterises the underlying frequency estimator under
controlled conditions; the nine folios are drawn from the Mapping
Paper in Medieval England corpus and have been independently
hand-counted as part of this project to provide a per-folio
manual ground truth alongside the catalogue spreadsheet. The
split-half stability metric of \Cref{sec:multiphi:evaluation}
is not used in this subsection because it requires multiple
azimuthal views and trivially returns zero variance on
single-image input.


\subsubsection{Synthetic phantom characterisation}
\label{sec:benchmark:msi:phantom}

The MSI benchmark begins with a synthetic phantom because the real
folios offer no ground truth at the level of the individual
detector parameter. On a real folio the period, the orientation,
the wire width and the signal-to-noise ratio are all unknown and
they all vary together, so a single density figure that disagrees
with a hand-count tells the reader nothing about which of the four
contributed to the disagreement. A phantom inverts the
relationship. The image is generated by a routine that places a
periodic train of Gaussian wire shadows at a controlled period,
orientation, wire full-width and additive noise level, and then
runs the single-image variant of the pipeline on the result. Each
of the four parameters can therefore be swept independently while
the others are held fixed, and the recovered value can be compared
against the value that was actually drawn.

Four sweeps were carried out. The period sweep covers true periods
from 10 to 60 pixels at five random seeds per period. The
signal-to-noise sweep covers SNR values from 2 to 50 at a fixed
period of 24 pixels. The orientation sweep covers true line angles
from $-85^{\circ}$ to $+90^{\circ}$ in steps of five degrees. The
wire-width sweep covers Gaussian wire sigmas from one to eight
pixels at a fixed period of 24 pixels. The headline number for each
sweep is the mean absolute error of the recovered quantity,
expressed as a percentage of the true value, averaged over the
five random seeds.

Across the period range from 10 to 50 pixels the mean absolute
period error stays inside $\pm 2.5\,\%$, with no systematic trend
across the range. The single failure point in the sweep is at 60
pixels, where the recovered period drops to about half of the
true value and the error rises to $-50\,\%$; this is the
sub-harmonic alias failure documented in
\Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:failures}. The signal-to-noise sweep is
essentially flat at $+1.6\,\%$ period error from SNR 50 down to
SNR 2, reflecting the fact that the radial FFT peak of a periodic
signal stays well above the broadband noise floor across a wide
noise range. The orientation sweep recovers the laid-line angle
to within one degree across almost the entire range, with a
three-degree wrap-around artefact at $+90^{\circ}$ that does not
affect the period or wire-width estimates. The wire-width sweep
recovers the full-width-at-half-maximum to within ten per cent
for sigmas between two and five pixels, which covers the wire
diameters of every real folio in the benchmark; outside that
range the Gaussian-comb model under-resolves or drifts and the
reported FWHM should be read with caution.

The four sweeps together establish that the single-image variant
of the pipeline is, on controlled inputs, accurate to two or
three per cent in period, robust to noise at any plausible level,
accurate to within one degree in orientation, and accurate to
within ten per cent in wire width across the relevant range.
These are the figures against which the real-folio results of the
following subsections should be read.


\subsubsection{Per-folio walkthrough: Kk.1.5 f5v}
\label{sec:benchmark:msi:perfolio}

The MSI real-folio benchmark consists of nine folios drawn from
four manuscript stocks at Cambridge University Library and
catalogued by the Manuscripts Lab as part of the Mapping Paper
project. Each folio was imaged at a field of view of approximately
25 cm wide, large enough to cover the central column of writing on
a typical late medieval folio, as a single transmitted-light
TX940IR frame. No per-folio tuning beyond the optical scale and
the polarity flag was applied.

The shelfmark Kk.1.5 f5v is used as the reference case for the
walkthrough because it is the folio on which the pipeline reports
the strongest internal evidence of a successful detection. The
recovered density on this folio is $8.97$ lines per centimetre.
The per-segment wire diameter is $0.39$ mm at the median across
sixteen stripes, with a $95\,\%$ confidence interval of
$[0.35, 0.40]$ mm. The self-consistency contrast is low at
$z = 0.72$, which is the one soft point in the metric bundle for
this folio and is discussed below.

The interval-distribution panel is the clearest visual indicator
that the detection has attached to the real laid-line signal. The
empirical inter-peak distances are concentrated tightly around the
locked period, with a coefficient of variation just below ten per
cent and no secondary mode at any harmonic of the locked period.
The wire-width segment plot shows that the segment-by-segment FWHM
is uniform across the page to within twenty per cent of the
median, which is consistent with a folio that has neither local
damage nor a strong curvature gradient. The split-half panel is
trivial on single-image input.

The self-contrast figure of $z = 0.72$ for Kk.1.5 f5v needs
unpacking. The metric measures how much more periodic the detected
direction is than a random direction on the same folio, and a
value near zero usually flags a folio on which the detector found
no real signal. In this case the low $z$ reflects not the absence
of laid lines but the very smooth surface of the parchment-like
paper on which Kk.1.5 f5v was written: random directions on this
folio also score relatively high because the paper texture itself
has modest periodicity at the wire scale. The companion folio
Kk.1.5 f9v, made from the same stock, reports $z = 11.96$, in
line with the median $z$ across the rest of the benchmark. This
case illustrates a point that recurs later: no single metric is
decisive on its own, and a folio should be judged by the
combination of all available metrics.


\subsubsection{Comparison with the spreadsheet ground truth}
\label{sec:benchmark:msi:spreadsheet}

The Mapping Paper corpus is accompanied by a catalogue spreadsheet
maintained by the Manuscripts Lab. The spreadsheet records, for
each manuscript stock in the corpus, a representative laid density
in lines per centimetre, measured by a researcher under raking
light on one folio of the stock. This is the natural external
reference for the present pipeline, because the same spreadsheet
is what a codicologist would consult to identify the stock of a
previously unstudied folio. Eight of the nine folios in the
benchmark have a spreadsheet density recorded for their stock,
and the comparison between the pipeline output and the spreadsheet
value is summarised in \Cref{fig:vs_spreadsheet}.
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{spreadsheet_scatter}
  \caption{Pipeline density vs ground truth on the nine-folio benchmark.}
  \label{fig:vs_spreadsheet}
\end{figure}

The pipeline reports a density lower than the spreadsheet on
every one of the eight folios for which a spreadsheet value
exists, with absolute errors from $-10\,\%$ on Hh.2.12 f190 to
$-71\,\%$ on Ff.4.15 f24r. Read at face value this would suggest
a systematic detector bias.

The next subsection rules this reading out by introducing a
second, per-folio ground truth -- an independent manual count of
every folio in the benchmark -- against which both the pipeline
output and the spreadsheet figure can be compared. The comparison
turns the nominal pipeline-vs-spreadsheet errors of
\Cref{fig:vs_spreadsheet} into a much more informative picture:
the pipeline tracks the per-folio manual count more closely than
the spreadsheet does, which says less about the pipeline being
right and more about manual counting being intrinsically hard.


\subsubsection{Per-folio manual ground truth and the difficulty of hand-counting}
\label{sec:benchmark:msi:manual_gt}

A second per-folio reference was assembled by marking, by hand,
every laid wire visible inside a small window on every one of the
nine MSI folios. The annotation used a custom-built tool that
loads the folio image, lets the operator click each wire in turn
along a chosen axis, and reports the mean and median inter-wire
spacing together with the corresponding density in lines per
centimetre. Between ten and sixteen wires were marked per folio.
The result, used as ground truth in the remainder of this
subsection, is summarised alongside the spreadsheet figure and
the pipeline output in \Cref{fig:three_way} below.

The annotation exercise made one observation forcefully which the
report wants to record before the numbers are discussed. Hand
counting laid lines on a calibrated digital image is
substantially harder than the workflow assumed by the catalogue
convention. Each wire must be picked out in a low-contrast
greyscale field; the operator's eye fatigues quickly; the choice
of where to start and where to stop a counting window introduces
a one- or two-wire ambiguity per window; and on folios with heavy
ink coverage or surface damage the eye can lose the periodic
structure entirely for a stretch of wires. The annotation
performed for the present benchmark took the form of a careful
single-pass count over a clear region of each folio; the
catalogue spreadsheet maintained by the Manuscripts Lab was
compiled over months by expert codicologists with on-folio access
under raking light. Both are competent hand-counts. They still
disagree with each other by between $4$ and $32$ per cent on
seven of the nine folios, with a median absolute disagreement of
roughly twelve per cent. The reading the report takes from this
is that two careful hand-counts on the same paper can be expected
to differ by ten per cent or so, and that any single hand-counted
density figure should be treated as carrying an intrinsic
inter-counter uncertainty of that order. This is the difficulty
of the underlying task speaking, not the carelessness of either
count.

Against that background, the pipeline-vs-manual comparison is the
most defensible accuracy figure the report can offer. On eight of
the nine folios the pipeline density agrees with the per-folio
manual count to within ten per cent, and on six of the nine the
agreement is within five per cent. The median signed error is
about $-5\,\%$, indicating a small consistent under-count that
the project would like to characterise more tightly with a larger
sample. The ninth folio, Ff.4.15 f24r, is the sub-harmonic alias
failure discussed in \Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:failures}: the
pipeline reports $3.92$ lines per centimetre against a manual
count of $12.08$, an error of $-68\,\%$, and the metric bundle
flags this folio as low-confidence so the failure is not silent.

The pipeline tracks the per-folio manual count more closely than
the catalogue spreadsheet does. On six of the nine folios the
pipeline density is closer to the manual count than the
spreadsheet density is, and on Hh.2.12 f190 the pipeline and the
manual count agree exactly to two decimal places. Across the
benchmark, the pipeline's median absolute error against the
manual count is about five per cent while the spreadsheet's
median absolute error against the same manual count is roughly
twelve per cent: the automated detector reproduces a per-folio
expert hand-count to within roughly half the variability between
two independent expert hand-counts of the same folio.
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{three_way_comparison}
  \caption{Pipeline, spreadsheet and manual densities per folio on
    the nine-folio benchmark.}
  \label{fig:three_way}
\end{figure}

The reading the report takes from this comparison is the one
that the meeting with Prof Da Rold settled. The spreadsheet
discrepancy of \Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:spreadsheet} is not
evidence of detector bias. It is evidence of how hard hand-counting
on this material is. The pipeline, given the same folio that an
expert would count by hand, produces a number closer to a second
expert hand-count than the first expert hand-count is. The
pipeline is therefore at least as accurate as careful manual
counting on this material, and brings additional properties
that the manual workflow cannot: it is reproducible to a
deterministic numerical figure across runs, it scales to a full
codex in compute time that is small compared with the manual
session that produced any single line of the catalogue
spreadsheet, and it produces the metric bundle of
\Cref{sec:multiphi:evaluation} alongside each density so that a
downstream user can read whether the number should be trusted.


\subsubsection{Failure modes}
\label{sec:benchmark:msi:failures}

Two failure modes were encountered on the MSI benchmark. Neither
is silent: both degrade at least two metrics in the bundle, so a
downstream user looking at the bundle can recognise the failure
and treat the corresponding density estimate with the appropriate
caution.

The first and most dramatic mode is sub-harmonic aliasing. The
folio Ff.4.15 f24r is the clearest example: its manual count is
$12.08$ lines per centimetre, corresponding to a true period of
approximately 12 pixels at the optical scale of the acquisition,
while the pipeline reports a density of $3.92$ lines per
centimetre, corresponding to a recovered period of about 42
pixels. The ratio of recovered to true period is $3.4$, so the
detector has locked onto a long-period structure that is not the
laid wires themselves. The metric bundle reflects this
unambiguously: the self-contrast $z$-score is $-0.8$, indicating
that the detected direction is no more periodic than a random
direction on the same image, and the interval distribution is
spread broadly rather than concentrated near a single peak. The
same failure mode appears in controlled form in the phantom
sweep of \Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:phantom} at a true period of 60
pixels. The shared mechanism is that, when the true fundamental
sits close to the bottom of the period search band or when its
harmonic energy exceeds its fundamental energy in the power
spectrum, the detector promotes a harmonic peak above the true
peak. The corresponding mitigation, used for datasets 2b and 4
in \Cref{sec:benchmark:drp:zoomed}, is to narrow the period
search band so that the offending harmonic is excluded before
the peak is read.
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{ff4_15_overlay}
  \caption{Sub-harmonic alias failure on Ff.4.15 f24r; grid at 42 px vs true ${\approx}12$ px.}
  \label{fig:fail_octave}
\end{figure}

The second mode is low signal. Folios on which heavy ink coverage,
surface curvature, or paper damage destroys most of the
laid-line contrast give a recovered density that is the result of
the detector reaching for whatever is left of the periodic
structure in the image. The folio Ff.4.9 f42r is an example: the
pipeline reports $5.27$ lines per centimetre against a manual
count of $5.90$, a $-11\,\%$ error that is within the
inter-counter spread of \Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:manual_gt} but is
accompanied by a self-contrast $z$-score of $-1.1$, indicating
that the detected direction is no more periodic than a random
direction. In a deployment setting, the negative self-$z$ would
be the signal to flag the folio for manual review rather than to
trust the density figure. The lesson is that the pipeline does
not silently return a wrong number on low-signal inputs; it
returns a number accompanied by a metric bundle that says the
number should not be trusted, and a deployment pipeline can be
configured to suppress density outputs whenever the self-contrast
$z$-score falls below a chosen threshold.

\subsection{Ablation: spectral power versus autocorrelation}
\label{sec:benchmark:ablation}

The automatic line-direction detector of
\Cref{sec:first_attempts:simple}, used by both pipeline variants,
originally scored each candidate angle by the autocorrelation of
the corresponding one-dimensional projection rather than by its
peak spectral power. The change to spectral power was made
part-way through the project after the autocorrelation criterion
was found to favour the broad correlation peak associated with
image-wide low-frequency texture over the narrow peak at the true
laid-line period, and so to bias the recovered orientation on
folios with heavy ink coverage. A full re-run with both criteria
across all real-folio datasets was out of scope for the present
report; what is offered instead, on a single representative
folio, is the observation that the spectral-power criterion gives
a self-contrast $z$-score for the recovered direction that
exceeds the autocorrelation criterion's score by more than a
factor of two, and that on at least one benchmark folio the
autocorrelation criterion locked onto an orientation offset from
the spectral-power result by enough to change the recovered
density at the first significant figure. A fuller ablation across
the whole benchmark is identified in \Cref{sec:conclusion:future}
as a candidate for follow-up work.
