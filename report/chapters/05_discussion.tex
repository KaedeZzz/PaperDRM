\subsection{Interpreting the spreadsheet discrepancy}
\label{sec:discussion:spreadsheet}

The most striking pattern in the real folio benchmark of
\Cref{sec:benchmark} is the gap between the pipeline and the
catalogue spreadsheet on seven of the nine MSI folios, with
errors ranging from 10\% to 70\%. The natural first
reading is that the spreadsheet is the authoritative reference
and the pipeline is wrong by varying amounts; that reading is
ruled out by the manual count of
\Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:manual_gt}, which agrees with the pipeline
more closely than it agrees with the spreadsheet on six of the
nine folios.

The interpretation that makes the comparison coherent is that the
spreadsheet figure is itself a careful hand count and therefore
carries the same intrinsic difficulty as any other hand count on
this material. The two independent hand counts of the present
benchmark, that is, the catalogue spreadsheet compiled by the
Manuscripts Lab and the per-folio re-count made for this report,
differ from each other by a median of roughly 12\%
across the nine folios, with individual disagreements as large as
30\%. That spread is the inter-counter variability of
laid-line counting on real manuscript paper, and the pipeline
output sits inside it rather than outside it.

A secondary point follows from the same observation. The
catalogue spreadsheet records one density per manuscript stock,
measured on one folio of the stock; a stock is a batch of paper
with one papermaker and one approximate production period, but
not in general one pair of moulds. Some of the 
disagreement between counts of the same folio above will therefore reflect real
density variation between folios cut from different sheets of the
same batch, rather than measurement noise alone. Distinguishing
the two contributions would require manual counts on additional
folios of the same stock, which is identified in
\Cref{sec:conclusion:future} as the most valuable extension of
the present project.

The practical implication is that a density match within a few
percent against a catalogue spreadsheet entry should be treated
as approximate agreement rather than as a confirmed match, and
that a folio specific comparison of the kind enabled by the present
pipeline that produces a deterministic, reproducible number
on every folio in a few minutes is a more faithful basis for
stock or mould pair identification than reliance on a single
catalogue figure.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

The pipeline has four limitations that constrain its present use
and would each encourage further work; each is noted here together
with the metric or experimental observation that exposes it.

The first is sub-harmonic aliasing at densities near the boundary
of the period search band, demonstrated by the phantom sweep at a
true period of 60 pixels and by the real folio Ff.4.15 f24r in
\Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:failures}. The mitigation employed in the
benchmark, narrowing the laid line period range when a previous
run shows signs of aliasing, requires a human in the loop and
defeats the no-tuning ambition stated in
\Cref{sec:bg:contributions}. A more robust mitigation would
detect the alias automatically by comparing the energy of the
recovered peak with the energy of its half and double, and would
demote the peak whenever a stronger candidate is present at a
harmonically related period.

The second is the wire width estimator, which the phantom sweep
of \Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:phantom} shows is accurate to within 10\%
 only for true sigmas between two and five pixels. Outside
that range the Gaussian comb model that underpins the estimator
either overlooks the wire or fails to find a clean inter-wire
baseline. In the present benchmark, the laid densities of every
folio fall comfortably inside the accurate range, so the wire
diameter is a usable secondary feature, but a pipeline applied to
unusually fine or unusually coarse paper would need a different
model for the wire profile.

The third is the polarity flag \texttt{wire\_is\_darker}, which
must be set by the user according to whether the input is
reflective or transmitted light. The half-period auto correction
of \Cref{sec:multiphi:final} removes any ambiguity within the
reflective acquisitions used in the present benchmark, but does
not by itself decide whether the wires should be expected to be
dark or bright. An automatic polarity detector that uses the
broadband signal statistics, identified as future work in
\Cref{sec:conclusion}, would remove the last piece of folio specific
user input from the pipeline.

The fourth is the optical scale. The pipeline reports density in
lines per centimetre by combining the recovered period in pixels
with a field-of-view width supplied in the configuration
file. A wrong field-of-view value propagates linearly into the
reported density. The current workflow uses the value recorded by
the AddME Lab at acquisition time, but no physical scale
verification is built into the pipeline. A small calibration
target inside the acquired frame, or a separate scale-bar image
per folio, would close this loop and remove an external dependency
that the rest of the pipeline cannot detect when it is wrong.

\subsection{Comparison with prior art}
\label{sec:discussion:prior_art}

The closest predecessor to the present work in the published
literature is the projection-Fourier family of laid line detectors
typified by Hiary and Ng~\cite{hiary2007paper} and surveyed in
\Cref{sec:bg:prior_extraction}. Those methods compute a
one-dimensional power spectrum of the column-mean of a
transmitted light or reflected light image at a user-supplied
orientation and read off the dominant peak. They are simple, fast,
and reasonably robust on clean transmitted light scans.

The pipelines of the present project differ from the prior
projection-Fourier family in three respects that apply to both
variants. First, the line orientation is not supplied by the user
but recovered from the image itself by the spectral-power scan
described in \Cref{sec:first_attempts:simple}, removing the
orientation as a parameter that has to be set per folio. Second,
each density estimate is accompanied by the metric bundle of
\Cref{sec:multiphi:evaluation}, which makes the confidence in the
estimate inspectable rather than tacit. Third, the
single image MSI variant of \Cref{sec:multiphi:single} reproduces
the per folio manual count of \Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:manual_gt} more
closely than the catalogue spreadsheet does on six of nine folios,
which places the detector inside the inter-counter variability
of careful hand counting on this material -- a stronger
agreement than the accuracies typically reported for
projection-Fourier methods on similar imagery. The multi-phi DRP variant
extends the same framework with a coherent summation across
azimuthal views, described in \Cref{sec:multiphi:aggregate}, that
trades a longer runtime for each folio and the requirement of
multi-angle imaging for the additional split-half stability metric
and the controlled sample confidence demonstrated on dataset 10 in
\Cref{sec:benchmark:drp:zoomed}.

The other relevant line of prior work is the spectral total
variation approach of Grossmann et al., which extracts chain and
laid features from a single reflected light image by a denoising
step rather than by frequency analysis~\cite{grossmann2023chain}. That
method is complementary rather than directly comparable, because
it produces an enhanced image that a human reader still interprets
visually, whereas both variants of the present pipeline return a
numerical density estimate with a quantified uncertainty. A
productive direction for future work would combine the two: use a
total variation decomposition as the preprocessing input to either
the single image or the multi-phi detector, in the hope that the
cleaned image would push the self-contrast $z$-score above its
current threshold on the low-signal folios identified in
\Cref{sec:benchmark:msi:failures}.

\subsection{Implications for codicology}
\label{sec:discussion:codicology}

The pipeline's folio-specific agreement with a careful manual count,
to the magnitude within roughly half the inter-counter variability of two
independent hand counts on the same paper, is sufficient in
principle to discriminate the laid densities of distinct mould
pairs in handmade European paper. The density
variation within a single mould expected from a single papermaker working with a single
mould pair is of the order of one wire per centimetre across
normal handling, and the inter-mould variation within the same
workshop is typically larger. A detector whose error
sits inside the inter-counter spread of expert hand counting
therefore meets the threshold at which mould pair identification
becomes possible from density alone, without the additional
features (chain-line spacing, watermark geometry) that the
classical codicological workflow relies on.

The same accuracy, combined with the runtime of a few
minutes for each folio on commodity hardware, makes whole-codex analysis tractable
in a way that manual counting cannot match. A typical late
medieval codex contains between two hundred and four hundred
folios. A manual count under raking light on every folio of such a
codex is a specialist task that takes multiple days and exposes the binding to
repeated handling; the single-image MSI variant of the present
pipeline could process the same codex in under a day on a laptop
from the kind of MSI archive that already exists at the major
holding libraries, and the multi-phi DRP variant could process it
with stronger confidence on the subset of folios for which a DRP
acquisition is available. Either path produces a laid line
density together with its metric bundle. The aggregate density profile of a codex,
folio by folio, would itself be a new kind of codicological
object, with the potential to surface mould changes mid-codex that
a per-stock spreadsheet would average over.

A second, more cautious implication concerns the meaning of
existing per-stock density figures in published catalogues. The
within-stock spread observed in
\Cref{sec:discussion:spreadsheet} suggests that catalogue
densities should be read as approximate stock signatures rather
than as folio specifications, and that any density based
inference of stock membership should allow for the spread
empirically observed in this benchmark. Putting more calibrated
folio data into the literature would, over time, replace the
"single figure per stock" convention with a "distribution per stock"
convention that more faithfully represents the underlying physical
variation.

\subsection{Environmental, social and ethical considerations}
\label{sec:discussion:ethics}

The environmental and social footprint of the work is small and
unambiguously positive: one run for a single folio takes a few watt-hours of
laptop compute and avoids the physical handling that the manual
workflow requires, and the same pipeline running on existing MSI
archives lowers the barrier to quantitative paper analysis for
institutions without a resident codicologist.

The ethical dimension of the work centres on cultural-heritage
data ownership and on the framing of provenance results. The
imagery used in this project was acquired by the AddME Lab in
Cambridge from the Mapping Paper in Medieval England corpus
maintained by the Manuscripts Lab, and remains subject to the
licence of Cambridge University Library; this project did not
redistribute any folio image outside its institutional licence.
On the framing of results, paper-provenance work intersects with
restitution and acquisition history claims that have
consequences beyond academic codicology, and the laid line density
estimates that the pipeline produces should be reported with their
metric bundles intact rather than as bare numbers. The metric
bundle is the part of the present contribution that most directly
serves the ethical purpose: it makes the quantified uncertainty
visible alongside the laid line density estimate, and therefore makes it
harder for a codicologist to overclaim from a low confidence
detection. Replacing qualitative counts with metric bundled
estimates is in this sense itself an ethical improvement on the academic
status quo.
