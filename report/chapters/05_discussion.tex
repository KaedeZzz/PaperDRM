\subsection{Interpreting the spreadsheet discrepancy}
\label{sec:discussion:spreadsheet}

The most striking pattern in the real-folio benchmark of
\Cref{sec:benchmark} is the gap between the agreement on the two
manually counted folios, where the pipeline matches the per-folio
manual count to better than one per cent, and the disagreement on
the other seven folios, where the pipeline disagrees with the
stock-level spreadsheet density by between ten and seventy per
cent. The natural first reading is that the spreadsheet is the
authoritative reference and the pipeline is wrong by varying
amounts on seven folios. The strong agreement on the calibrated
pair rules this reading out, because it would be implausible for
the pipeline to be wrong by tens of per cent on seven folios while
being right to within one per cent on a different two with no
parameter change in between.

The alternative reading, supported by the structure of the corpus
itself, is that the spreadsheet records a stock-level density that
need not match the density of any specific folio drawn from the
stock. A manuscript stock in the Mapping Paper corpus is a batch of
paper that shares one papermaker and one approximate production
period, but it does not in general share a single pair of moulds.
The catalogue spreadsheet was assembled by counting one folio per
stock under raking light. The resulting density is a representative
value, not a per-folio specification, and any folio of the same
stock that came from a different mould pair will have a measurably
different density.

The implication for the field is concrete. A codicologist who
wants to verify the stock of a previously unstudied folio against
the spreadsheet should expect a within-stock spread of the same
order as the present benchmark's seven discrepancies. The pipeline
is not introducing this spread; it is detecting it. A density
match within a few per cent against the spreadsheet stock value is
therefore weaker evidence than has historically been treated as a
match, and a per-folio comparison of the present kind is a more
faithful basis for stock or mould-pair identification.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

The pipeline has four limitations that constrain its present use
and would each repay further work. Each is named here together
with the metric or experimental observation that exposes it.

The first is sub-harmonic aliasing at densities near the boundary
of the period search band, demonstrated by the phantom sweep at a
true period of 60 pixels and by the real folio Ff.4.15 f24r in
\Cref{sec:benchmark:failures}. The mitigation employed in the
benchmark, narrowing the per-folio period range when a previous
run shows signs of aliasing, requires a human in the loop and
defeats the no-tuning ambition stated in
\Cref{sec:bg:contributions}. A more robust mitigation would
detect the alias automatically by comparing the energy of the
recovered peak with the energy of its half and double, and would
demote the peak whenever a stronger candidate is present at a
harmonically related period.

The second is the wire-width estimator, which the phantom sweep
of \Cref{sec:benchmark:phantom} shows is accurate to within ten
per cent only for true sigmas between two and five pixels. Outside
that range the Gaussian-comb model that underpins the estimator
either under-resolves the wire or fails to find a clean inter-wire
baseline. In the present benchmark the laid densities of every
folio fall comfortably inside the accurate range, so the wire
diameter is a usable secondary feature, but a pipeline applied to
unusually fine or unusually coarse paper would need a different
model for the wire profile.

The third is the polarity flag \texttt{wire\_is\_darker}, which
must be set by the user according to whether the input is
reflective or transmitted light. The half-period auto-correction
of \Cref{sec:multiphi:final} removes any ambiguity within the
reflective acquisitions used in the present benchmark, but does
not by itself decide whether the wires should be expected to be
dark or bright. An automatic polarity detector that uses the
broadband signal statistics, identified as future work in
\Cref{sec:conclusion}, would remove the last piece of per-folio
user input from the pipeline.

The fourth is the optical scale. The pipeline reports density in
lines per centimetre by combining the recovered period in pixels
with a per-folio field-of-view width supplied in the configuration
file. A wrong field-of-view value propagates linearly into the
reported density. The current workflow uses the value recorded by
the AddME Lab at acquisition time, but no per-folio scale
verification is built into the pipeline. A small calibration
target inside the acquired frame, or a separate scale-bar image
per folio, would close this loop and remove an external dependency
that the rest of the pipeline cannot detect when it is wrong.

\subsection{Comparison with prior art}
\label{sec:discussion:prior_art}

The closest predecessor to the present work in the published
literature is the projection-Fourier family of laid-line detectors
typified by Hiary and Ng~\cite{TODO_hiary_ng} and surveyed in
\Cref{sec:bg:prior_extraction}. Those methods compute a
one-dimensional power spectrum of the column-mean of a
transmitted-light or reflected-light image at a user-supplied
orientation and read off the dominant peak. They are simple, fast,
and reasonably robust on clean transmitted-light scans.

The multi-phi pipeline of the present project differs from the
prior projection-Fourier family in three respects. First, the
orientation is not supplied by the user but recovered from the
DRP stack itself by the spectral-power scan described in
\Cref{sec:first_attempts:simple}, removing the orientation as a
parameter that has to be set per folio. Second, the spectrum from
which the period is read is not a single per-image spectrum but
the aggregated spectrum of \Cref{sec:multiphi:aggregate} across
all available azimuthal views, which converts the multiple grazing
images that a DRP acquisition naturally provides into a
signal-to-noise advantage. Third, each density estimate is
accompanied by the metric bundle of
\Cref{sec:multiphi:evaluation}, which makes the confidence in the
estimate inspectable rather than tacit. The cost of these
differences is a longer per-folio runtime and a dependence on
multi-angle imaging rather than a single transmitted-light scan;
the gain is the sub-one-per-cent accuracy demonstrated in
\Cref{sec:benchmark:manual_gt}.

The other relevant line of prior work is the spectral total
variation approach of Grossmann et al., which extracts chain and
laid features from a single reflected-light image by a denoising
step rather than by frequency analysis~\cite{TODO_grossmann}. That
method is complementary rather than directly comparable, because
it produces an enhanced image that a human reader still interprets
visually, whereas the present pipeline returns a numerical density
estimate with a quantified uncertainty. A productive direction for
future work would combine the two: use a total-variation
decomposition as the preprocessing input to the multi-phi
aggregator, in the hope that the cleaned image would push the
self-contrast $z$-score above its current threshold on the
low-signal folios identified in \Cref{sec:benchmark:failures}.

\subsection{Implications for codicology}
\label{sec:discussion:codicology}

The headline accuracy figure of sub-one per cent at the folio level
is, taken at face value, sufficient to discriminate the laid
densities of distinct mould pairs in handmade European paper. The
within-mould density variation expected from a single papermaker
working with a single mould pair is of the order of one wire per
centimetre across normal handling, and the inter-mould variation
within the same workshop is typically larger. A detector whose
per-folio error is comfortably below one wire per centimetre
therefore meets the threshold at which mould-pair identification
becomes possible from density alone, without the additional
features (chain-line spacing, watermark geometry) that the
classical codicological workflow relies on.

The same accuracy, combined with the per-folio runtime of a few
minutes on commodity hardware, makes whole-codex analysis tractable
in a way that manual counting cannot match. A typical late
medieval codex contains between two hundred and four hundred
folios. A manual count under raking light on every folio of such a
codex is a multi-day specialist task that exposes the binding to
repeated handling; the present pipeline could process the same
codex in under a day on a laptop, given a pre-existing DRP
acquisition, and would produce a per-folio density together with
its metric bundle. The aggregate density profile of a codex,
folio by folio, would itself be a new kind of codicological
object, with the potential to surface mould changes mid-codex that
a per-stock spreadsheet would average over.

A second, more cautious implication concerns the meaning of
existing per-stock density figures in published catalogues. The
within-stock spread observed in
\Cref{sec:discussion:spreadsheet} suggests that catalogue
densities should be read as approximate stock signatures rather
than as folio-level specifications, and that any density-based
inference of stock membership should allow for the spread
empirically observed in this benchmark. Putting more calibrated
per-folio data into the literature would, over time, replace the
single-figure-per-stock convention with a distribution-per-stock
convention that more faithfully represents the underlying physical
variation.

\subsection{Environmental, social and ethical considerations}
\label{sec:discussion:ethics}

The environmental footprint of the pipeline is small. A full
multi-phi run plus the five evaluation metrics takes between three
and ten minutes per folio on a laptop CPU, and the corresponding
energy cost is on the order of a few watt-hours. Even at the
codex-scale workflow envisaged in the previous subsection, the
compute energy is small compared with the alternative of repeated
physical handling under raking light, which requires controlled
laboratory conditions, transport of fragile material, and the time
of skilled personnel. The dominant environmental cost of the
underlying workflow is the DRP acquisition itself, which uses a
microscope and a controllable light source over a session of order
ten minutes per folio. The acquisition energy is also modest, and
the per-folio amortisation improves further every time the same
DRP stack is reanalysed with an improved pipeline.

The social value of the pipeline is in lowering the barrier to
quantitative paper analysis for institutions that hold manuscript
collections but do not host a resident codicologist. The
historical workflow requires on-site specialist access to the
physical folio; the present workflow requires only a calibrated
DRP scan and a laptop. An institution that captures a DRP scan
once can, in principle, share that scan with collaborators
anywhere in the world for quantitative analysis, expanding the
community of researchers who can contribute to mould-pair
attributions. The same property carries a dual-use risk: the same
density signature that helps confirm provenance can in principle
support attribution claims used for art-market authentication, with
consequences that go beyond pure scholarship. The mitigation
adopted in the present project is that all analysis is performed
on imagery licensed by the holding institution and that no
imagery is redistributed.

The ethical dimension of the work centres on cultural-heritage
data ownership and on the framing of provenance results. The
imagery used in this project was acquired by the AddME Lab in
Cambridge from the Mapping Paper in Medieval England corpus
maintained by the Manuscripts Lab, and remains subject to the
licence of Cambridge University Library; this project did not
redistribute any folio image outside its institutional licence.
On the framing of results, paper-provenance work intersects with
restitution and acquisition-history claims that have
consequences beyond academic codicology, and the per-folio density
estimates that the pipeline produces should be reported with their
metric bundles intact rather than as bare numbers. The metric
bundle is the part of the present contribution that most directly
serves the ethical purpose: it makes the quantified uncertainty
visible alongside the density estimate, and therefore makes it
harder for a downstream user to over-claim from a low-confidence
detection. Replacing qualitative counts with metric-bundled
estimates is in this sense itself an ethical improvement on the
status quo.
